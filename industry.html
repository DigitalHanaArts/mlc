Shape of the industry

The blockchain industry is not one thing. It is at least five distinct sectors that share a vocabulary and almost nothing else. Separating them is the first step to evaluating any claim about "blockchain".

  • Base-layer protocols. Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana, and a long tail of others. They provide the ledger itself, and sell block space.
  • Infrastructure. Wallets, node providers, indexers, oracles, bridges. They make the base layer usable.
  • Applications. Decentralised exchanges, lending protocols, stablecoins, NFT marketplaces. They sit on top and generate real usage.
  • Enterprise ledgers. Hyperledger, Corda, Quorum, and consortium chains. They are permissioned and mostly invisible to the public.
  • Adjacent systems. Content-addressed storage (IPFS, Filecoin), verifiable computation, zero-knowledge rollups. They borrow the vocabulary but solve different problems.

Conflating these is the source of most confusion in the field. "Blockchain" as a synonym for "cryptocurrency" is like "database" as a synonym for "bank".

What actually works

Three genuine use cases have survived a decade of market cycles and regulatory attention. They are not glamorous, and they are not what most people picture when they hear the word.

Settlement

Cross-border payments, stablecoin rails, and interbank settlement are the clearest success. The volume is real. Stablecoins alone settle hundreds of billions of dollars per year. This is boring infrastructure, and boring infrastructure is where blockchains earn their keep.

Tamper-evident records

Supply chain provenance, certificate registries, and timestamping services use blockchains as a notary. The chain is not paying anyone; it is providing a receipt. The value is in the receipt, not in any token.

Permissioned consortium ledgers

Trade finance, insurance, and healthcare consortia use blockchains to share state between institutions that do not trust each other. The nodes are named. The consensus is BFT. The economics are contractual, not speculative. This sector is small and largely invisible, and it is where the technology has the clearest product-market fit.

Pattern

The projects that work treat the blockchain as infrastructure. The projects that fail treat it as the product.

Science, research, and provenance

Scientific reproducibility is one of the most promising non-financial applications of blockchains, and it has received the least attention from the industry. That is largely because the value is subtle and the audience is small.

The problem is well known: published results often cannot be reproduced, because the code, the data, the parameters, or the environment are missing or have drifted. Journals, funding agencies, and researchers themselves have all tried to fix this with mandates, checklists, and archiving services.

A blockchain adds one specific thing to that toolkit: a tamper-evident record. Not "the data is correct", but "the data is the same as when it was registered". For scientific work, that distinction is often exactly what is missing.

Existing efforts

CERN Analysis Preservation, the Open Science Framework, Zenodo, and institutional repositories all provide archival storage with persistent identifiers. They do not provide local, personal, cryptographically verifiable records that an individual researcher can hold independently.

Where MLabChain fits

MLabChain is not a base-layer protocol, not an enterprise consortium ledger, and not a cryptocurrency. It sits in the tamper-evident records category, specialised for one workflow: machine-learning training.

The unit it issues, Mera, is not a currency and does not pretend to be one. It is a local, transferable record of verified scientific work. Its "price" is whatever the work it represents is worth to the people who care about that work — which is usually a small number of collaborators and a long-term institutional interest in reproducibility.

Economics and governance — the honest part

Token economics is where the industry has produced the most elaborate theories and the least empirical evidence. Four observations are worth stating plainly.

  1. Most tokens have no reason to exist. A token is useful only if the system it belongs to requires a scarce digital resource. If the system works with a normal database and a normal payment rail, the token is a fundraising mechanism dressed up as a protocol feature.
  2. Governance tokens do not govern. In practice, most governance is exercised by a small number of large holders, or by a foundation. The token is a signalling device, not a decision mechanism.
  3. "Yield" is usually dilution or leverage. A 10% APY on a stablecoin is either a subsidy paid from a treasury, or a reward for taking a risk that is not priced in.
  4. Regulatory clarity is a prerequisite, not an afterthought. Treating a token as a financial instrument brings securities law, tax law, and AML rules. Not every project can survive that.

MLabChain chooses the position that requires the least regulatory surface: it does not sell anything, does not promise returns, does not list on exchanges, and does not present Mera as an investment. If you want to build a financial product on top of it, that is a separate decision, made by you, with your own counsel.

Outlook

The industry is in a consolidation phase. The speculative applications have largely failed. The infrastructural ones are growing slowly. The scientific and public-sector use cases are the least mature, and arguably the most interesting, because they are the ones where the "trustless verification" property actually solves a real problem.

MLabChain is a small bet on that direction. It does not try to be a currency, a platform, or a movement. It tries to be a useful tool for one person who needs to prove what they ran.

Take-away

The blockchain industry is mostly noise with a few genuinely useful pieces inside. The useful pieces are infrastructural, boring, and slow. That is where this project tries to sit.
